# Grading Rubric — Assignment 4
## Campus Bike-Share: Data Structures End to End

**Total: 100 points**

### How to use this rubric

This rubric is the grading authority. There is **no answer key**, and the
reference solutions document is one acceptable answer set rather than the
standard to match against. Each criterion below says what an answer must
*demonstrate*; it does not say what words it must contain.

Three rules apply throughout:

1. **Any correct approach earns the marks.** Where a question has several
   defensible answers, the criterion says so. Do not deduct because a student
   chose differently from the reference solution.
2. **Reasoning is the deliverable on every "justify", "derive" or "compare"
   part.** A correct conclusion with no argument earns the marks for the
   conclusion only. A well-argued answer that reaches a different defensible
   conclusion earns full marks.
3. **Code is graded on whether it works and meets the stated complexity.** Do
   not deduct for naming, formatting, comments, or for not using the library or
   idiom you would have used. Do deduct for faults that stop it working: wrong
   operator or direction, off-by-one, undefined name, a method that does not do
   what the author thinks.

## Q1. Choosing the right structure — 12 points

**(a) Choice and justification — 5 points**

- **5** — Names a structure whose cost profile fits the workload (a hash
  table/dict and a balanced BST are both defensible) AND justifies it from the
  access pattern: identifies that lookup dominates by orders of magnitude and
  that the report is rare, and trades the two against each other explicitly.
- **3** — Names a suitable structure and gives a partial justification: correct
  complexities, but no engagement with the *relative frequency* of the
  operations.
- **1** — Names a suitable structure with no justification, or a justification
  that is generic ("hash tables are fast").
- **0** — Names a structure whose cost profile does not fit (e.g. a plain list
  or linked list for 40,000 lookups) without acknowledging the cost.

**(b) Effect of hourly reporting — 4 points**

- **4** — Reaches a conclusion (either "no change" or "switch") and supports it
  with a **quantitative** comparison: estimates the cost of the report against
  the cost of the lookups at their stated frequencies.
- **2** — Reaches a defensible conclusion with a qualitative argument only.
- **0** — Answers yes or no without comparing costs.

*Both conclusions are correct if argued. Do not reward the reference answer's
conclusion over the other one.*

**(c) Complexities — 3 points**

- **3** — All four operations correct and consistent with the structure the
  student chose in (a) — including the report, whose cost differs by structure
  (O(n log n) for a hash table, O(n) for a tree).
- **2** — Three of four correct, or all four correct for a *different* structure
  than the one chosen in (a).
- **1** — Two correct.
- **0** — Fewer than two correct.

*Average case was asked for. Do not deduct for also giving worst case.*

## Q2. Hashing — 14 points

**(a) Chaining vs open addressing — 4 points**

- **2 pts** — The mechanism of both is described correctly: chaining stores
  collisions in a secondary structure at the slot; open addressing stores all
  entries in the table and probes for a free slot.
- **1 pt** — A genuine advantage of chaining (tolerates α > 1, simple deletion,
  graceful degradation).
- **1 pt** — A genuine advantage of open addressing (no pointer overhead, cache
  locality/contiguous memory).

*An "advantage" that is just the restatement of the mechanism earns nothing.*

**(b) Load factor — 3 points**

- **1 pt** — α = 0.75.
- **2 pts** — Explains that expected probe length grows without bound as α → 1
  AND gives the mechanism: **primary clustering** — occupied slots merge into
  runs, any key hashing into a run must traverse it, and each insertion extends
  the run. Quoting the formula without the mechanism earns 1 of these 2.

**(c) Deletion under open addressing — 3 points**

- **2 pts** — Identifies that emptying a slot **breaks probe chains passing
  through it**, so keys stored beyond the hole become unfindable.
- **1 pt** — Names tombstones / deleted markers as the fix AND states a cost
  (they count toward the load factor, accumulate, require periodic rehashing).

*A student who says only "you must rehash the table" has a working fix and earns
the 1 point for it.*

**(d) Linear probing trace — 4 points**

Correct final table:

```
index:  0     1     2     3     4     5     6
value:  -    15     8    22    11     -    27
```

- **3 pts** — Final table exactly correct. Award 3 even if the working is not
  shown.
- **1 pt** — The probe sequences for 8 (1 → 2) and 22 (1 → 2 → 3) are shown.
- Partial: if the table is wrong, award **1** if at least three slots are right
  and the probing method is visibly correct — this part tests the procedure, so
  an arithmetic slip should not cost all four marks.

## Q3. The k nearest stations — 18 points

**(a) Implementation — 10 points**

- **6 pts** — **Correctness.** Returns the k nearest station ids, nearest
  first. Trace the logic; run it if you can. Deduct in full only for faults that
  break it (wrong comparison direction, using a min-heap where a max-heap is
  needed without compensating, returning objects where ids were asked for).
- **3 pts** — **Meets O(n log k).** The heap is bounded at k — the student
  evicts rather than accumulating. An O(n log n) solution that sorts everything
  is correct but does not meet the stated bound: award 0 of these 3, and full
  credit for the other criteria it satisfies.
- **1 pt** — **Edge cases.** `k <= 0` returns empty, and `k > n` does not crash.

*Full credit regardless of approach: a bounded max-heap, `heapq.nsmallest`, or
quickselect all qualify if the complexity bound holds. Squared distance instead
of `sqrt` is a nice optimisation but is **not** required and earns no extra
marks — nor does its absence cost any.*

**(b) Complexity justification — 4 points**

- **4** — States O(n log k) and accounts for it: n elements examined, each
  costing at most one O(log k) heap operation **because the heap is capped at
  k**. The causal link between the cap and the log k is what is being marked.
- **2** — States O(n log k) with a partial account, or accounts correctly for a
  different (correct) complexity matching their own code.
- **0** — States a complexity with no derivation, or one that does not match the
  code they wrote.

*If the student's code is O(n log n), the correct answer here is O(n log n).
Mark the consistency of the analysis with their own code.*

**(c) Heap vs sorting — 4 points**

- **2 pts** — Explains the saving concretely for n = 40,000, k = 5: log k ≈ 2.3
  against log n ≈ 15.3, and/or O(k) space against O(n). Numbers or a clear
  proportional argument both count.
- **2 pts** — Identifies that the advantage vanishes as **k approaches n**
  (at k = n the two are asymptotically equal). A student who argues sorting wins
  earlier in practice, on constant factors, earns these 2 as well.

## Q4. Shortest walking route — 20 points

**(a) Implementation — 10 points**

- **5 pts** — **Dijkstra is correct.** Maintains tentative distances, always
  settles the minimum unsettled vertex, relaxes edges from it.
- **3 pts** — **Returns the path, not only the distance**, via predecessor
  tracking or an equivalent, reconstructed in the right order (source → target).
- **2 pts** — **Handles the unreachable case** and returns the stated
  `(inf, [])` shape, or an equivalent the student documents.

*Both standard heap patterns are fully correct: pushing duplicate entries and
skipping stale pops, or maintaining a visited set with decrease-key. A student
who uses neither and re-scans for the minimum has written the O(V²) version —
that is a correct algorithm, so award these 10 marks in full and mark the
complexity in (b) against what they wrote.*

**(b) Complexity derivation — 6 points**

- **2 pts** — V pops from the heap, each O(log V).
- **2 pts** — E relaxations, each pushing at most once, O(log V) per push.
- **2 pts** — Combines to O((V + E) log V) **and the derivation matches the
  student's own implementation**.

*The question said "do not simply state the result", so a bare "O(E log V)" with
no working earns 0 even though it is the right answer. A correct derivation of
O(V²) for an array-based implementation, or O(V log V + E) for a Fibonacci heap,
earns full marks if that is what they wrote.*

**(c) Negative weights — 4 points**

- **2 pts** — A concrete three-node counterexample with edges and weights given,
  where a negative edge makes a longer-looking route cheaper.
- **1 pt** — States both what Dijkstra returns and the true shortest distance,
  and they are correct for the graph the student gave.
- **1 pt** — Names the violated assumption: once a vertex is settled its distance
  is treated as final, which only holds if extending a path cannot reduce its
  cost.

*Check the student's own graph for internal consistency rather than comparing it
to the reference example. Many valid counterexamples exist.*

## Q5. Amortized analysis — 12 points

**(a) Doubling — 6 points**

- **2 pts** — Writes the total copy cost as the geometric series
  1 + 2 + 4 + … + 2^k (or equivalent).
- **2 pts** — Evaluates it and shows the bound — the sum is < 2n, so the total
  work over n appends is O(n).
- **2 pts** — Concludes amortized O(1) per append, dividing total by n.

*Accept the accounting or potential method instead of the aggregate method if
correctly carried out, even though the question named the aggregate method.*

**(b) Fixed +10 growth — 6 points**

- **3 pts** — Derives the total copy cost as the arithmetic series
  10 + 20 + … + n ≈ n²/20, i.e. Θ(n²). The derivation must be present; the bare
  answer earns 1 of these 3.
- **1 pt** — States the resulting amortized cost per append: Θ(n).
- **2 pts** — Judges the trade and supports it: the memory saved is a constant
  factor while the time lost is a factor of n, so the proposal is bad. A student
  who identifies the general principle — geometric growth gives O(1), arithmetic
  growth gives O(n) — earns these 2 outright.

## Q6. Debugging — 12 points

**(a) Both faults — 6 points**

- **3 pts** — **Fault 1:** `hi = len(sorted_ids)` is a half-open bound used with
  the inclusive condition `lo <= hi`, allowing `mid` to reach `len` and index out
  of range.
- **3 pts** — **Fault 2:** `lo = mid` fails to exclude the midpoint, so once
  `hi == lo + 1` the interval stops shrinking and the loop does not terminate.

*Award per fault. Identifying the line without explaining the consequence earns
1 of the 3 for that fault. A student who reports a third "fault" that is not one
loses nothing, provided both real faults are among their answers.*

**(b) Masking and triggering inputs — 4 points**

- **2 pts** — Identifies that **fault 1 is hidden by fault 2** and explains why:
  `lo` advances only via `lo = mid`, which cannot reach `len`, so the loop hangs
  before any out-of-range index is reached. No input exposes fault 1 while fault
  2 is present.
- **1 pt** — A concrete input demonstrating the observable failure — any absent
  target above the first element, e.g. `[10, 20, 30]` with target 25 — and
  states that it hangs.
- **1 pt** — A concrete input that exposes fault 1 once fault 2 is repaired —
  any absent target greater than every element, e.g. `[10, 20, 30]` with target
  35 — and states that it raises `IndexError`.

*This is the hard part of the question and most students will miss the masking.
A student who gives two plausible-but-wrong trigger inputs, one per fault,
without noticing the masking, earns at most the 1 point for the hang.*

**(c) Corrected function — 2 points**

- **2** — Both faults fixed and the result is a correct binary search. The
  inclusive form (`hi = len - 1`, `while lo <= hi`, `hi = mid - 1`) and the
  half-open form (`hi = len`, `while lo < hi`, `hi = mid`) are equally correct.
- **1** — One fault fixed.
- **0** — Neither fixed, or the "fix" introduces a new fault.

## Q7. Designing a spatial query — 12 points

**No recommendation is required to be correct.** The marks are for the
comparison and the argument. A well-argued case for the linear scan and a
well-argued case for a spatial index both earn 12.

**Comparison of two approaches — 8 points**

Award per approach (4 points each), one of which must be the linear scan:

- **2 pts** — Query complexity stated and correct for that approach, with what
  it depends on (for a grid: the number of cells overlapping the radius and the
  bikes within them, not simply "O(1)").
- **1 pt** — Cost of a bike moving, correctly identified (free for the scan;
  constant-time bucket maintenance for an index).
- **1 pt** — Memory overhead, correctly identified.

**Recommendation — 4 points**

- **4** — Recommends one approach and argues it **against this specific
  workload** — engaging with at least two of: the 2 km radius against a 5 km
  campus, 500 queries/second, 40,000 objects, constant movement.
- **2** — Recommends one approach with a general argument that does not engage
  with the specific numbers.
- **0** — States a preference with no argument, or recommends nothing.

*The strongest available insight is that a 2 km radius on a 5 km campus returns
roughly half the data, and an index cannot prune what the query selects. Award
the full 4 for spotting it — but do not require it: an argument from query
throughput, from growth headroom, or from the cost of motion is equally valid,
and a k-d tree correctly rejected for rebalancing badly under constant movement
is also a full-credit line of reasoning.*
