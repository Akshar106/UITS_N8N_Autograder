CSCI-C343 Data Structures — Midterm Examination
Total: 100 points

Answer all five questions. Where pseudocode is requested, any clear notation is
acceptable; you are graded on the algorithm, not on syntax. State complexities
in Big-O and say what n (and V, E, W where relevant) refer to.

Q1. Binary Search Trees (20 points)

Define a binary search tree, give pseudocode for insertion, state the time
complexity of search, insertion and deletion, and explain the worst case.

  (a) 5 pts — The BST ordering property: for every node, all keys in the left
      subtree are less than the node's key and all keys in the right subtree
      are greater. Credit any equivalent statement of the invariant.
  (b) 5 pts — Correct insertion pseudocode: compare against the current node,
      descend left or right accordingly, insert at the empty position reached.
      Recursive or iterative both acceptable.
  (c) 5 pts — Search, insert and delete are O(log n) average and O(n) worst
      case. All three must be stated; full marks require both cases.
  (d) 5 pts — The worst case arises when the tree degenerates into a path
      (e.g. inserting already-sorted keys), leaving height n rather than
      log n. Requires naming a cause, not just asserting the bound.

Q2. Breadth-First Search (20 points)

Give pseudocode for BFS on an unweighted graph, analyse its complexity, and
contrast it with depth-first search.

  (a) 7 pts — Correct pseudocode: initialise a queue with the source, mark it
      visited, then repeatedly dequeue a vertex and enqueue each unvisited
      neighbour, marking it visited on enqueue. Marking on dequeue instead of
      enqueue is a defect worth at most 4 of the 7.
  (b) 5 pts — O(V + E) time, with a justification: every vertex is enqueued
      once and every edge examined once (twice for an undirected adjacency
      list). O(V) auxiliary space for the queue and visited set.
  (c) 4 pts — BFS finds shortest paths in an unweighted graph because it
      explores all vertices at distance k before any at distance k+1.
  (d) 4 pts — Contrast with DFS: stack (or recursion) instead of a queue,
      explores deeply before broadly, does not give shortest paths, and uses
      O(h) space where h is the depth reached.

Q3. Merge Sort and Recurrences (20 points)

Give pseudocode for merge sort, write and solve its recurrence, and state its
space complexity and stability.

  (a) 6 pts — Correct pseudocode: split the array in half, recurse on each
      half, merge the two sorted halves. A base case of length 0 or 1 must be
      present or implied.
  (b) 5 pts — The recurrence T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n), with the O(n) term
      identified as the cost of merging.
  (c) 4 pts — Solved to O(n log n), by the Master theorem or by observing
      log n levels each costing O(n).
  (d) 5 pts — O(n) auxiliary space for the merge buffer, and merge sort is
      stable provided the merge takes from the left half on ties.

Q4. Hash Tables (20 points)

Explain collision resolution, the role of the load factor, and why a dynamic
hash table achieves O(1) amortised insertion.

  (a) 5 pts — A collision is two distinct keys hashing to the same index.
      Separate chaining stores a list (or other container) of entries per
      bucket.
  (b) 5 pts — Open addressing probes for another slot within the table, with
      at least one named scheme: linear probing, quadratic probing, or double
      hashing.
  (c) 5 pts — The load factor α = n/m drives performance: chaining degrades to
      O(1 + α) per operation, open addressing degrades sharply as α approaches
      1, hence resizing at a threshold such as 0.75.
  (d) 5 pts — Amortised O(1) insertion: doubling capacity costs O(n) to rehash
      but happens only after n insertions, so the cost spread over those
      insertions is constant. Requires the argument, not just the phrase
      "amortised O(1)".

Q5. Dynamic Programming — 0/1 Knapsack (20 points)

Given n items with weights w[i] and values v[i], and a capacity W, give the
recurrence and pseudocode for the maximum attainable value, analyse the cost,
and explain why dynamic programming beats naive recursion here.

  (a) 6 pts — The recurrence:
        dp[i][c] = dp[i-1][c]                                  if w[i] > c
        dp[i][c] = max(dp[i-1][c], v[i] + dp[i-1][c - w[i]])   otherwise
      with dp[0][c] = 0. Equivalent formulations earn full credit.
  (b) 6 pts — Pseudocode filling the table in a valid order, returning
      dp[n][W]. A 1-D rolling array iterating capacity downward is equally
      correct and earns full marks.
  (c) 4 pts — O(nW) time and O(nW) space, or O(W) space with the rolling
      array.
  (d) 4 pts — Naive recursion re-solves overlapping subproblems and costs
      O(2^n); DP memoises them, and the answer should note that O(nW) is
      pseudo-polynomial because W is a numeric value, not an input length.
