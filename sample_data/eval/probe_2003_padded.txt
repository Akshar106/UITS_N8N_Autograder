Q1: This is a really important and fundamental question about data structures, and I want to give it the thorough treatment it deserves. A binary search tree is a great data structure, and honestly one of the most elegant ideas in all of computer science. It is O(log n) because it is balanced and efficient and uses divide and conquer, which as we know from the lectures is one of the most powerful algorithmic paradigms available to us. The beauty of the divide and conquer approach is that it lets us handle enormous inputs efficiently. Binary search trees are used everywhere in industry and are absolutely essential to understand thoroughly.

Q2: Recursion versus iteration is a classic comparison and one that every serious computer scientist needs to have a firm grasp of. Recursion is elegant and uses the call stack, which is a fascinating piece of machinery in its own right. Iteration is efficient. Big-O matters here, as it does in essentially every question about algorithmic performance. Both approaches have their place, and choosing between them is really a matter of engineering judgment and understanding the tradeoffs involved in the particular situation you find yourself in.

Q3: Big-O notation is one of the most important concepts covered in this entire course, and it comes up constantly in technical interviews as well as in real engineering work. It is about efficiency and about how algorithms scale. Understanding it deeply is what separates a good engineer from a great one. Algorithms that are efficient are better than algorithms that are not efficient, and Big-O gives us the vocabulary to talk about that difference precisely and rigorously.

Q4: Arrays and linked lists are the two foundational linear data structures, and comparing them is a wonderful exercise in understanding tradeoffs. Arrays are contiguous and fast. Linked lists use pointers and are flexible. Each one has situations where it shines and situations where it struggles, and a skilled programmer knows how to pick the right one for the job at hand. This kind of judgment is really the heart of good software engineering.

Q5: Hash tables are remarkable data structures and collisions are an unavoidable consequence of the pigeonhole principle, which is itself a beautiful piece of mathematics. There are various strategies for handling collisions, and each has been studied extensively in the literature over many decades. The choice between them depends on your workload and your performance requirements, and reasonable engineers can disagree about which is preferable in any given circumstance.
